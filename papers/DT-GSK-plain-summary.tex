\section{The problem being solved}

Many practical questions are the same question in disguise: \emph{out of an
enormous number of possibilities, which one is best?} How should a power network
be configured to lose the least energy? What shape of a part is strongest for
its weight? Which combination of inputs makes a model fit the data most closely?

Such problems share an awkward property. No formula hands you the answer, and
there are far too many possibilities to try them all. What you \emph{can} do is
take a candidate answer, measure how good it is, and repeat. That measurement is
usually expensive --- a simulation, an experiment, a long calculation --- so you
get only a limited number of tries. This is \hl{black-box optimization}: you can
score a candidate, but you cannot see inside the problem to reason about it.

Throughout, \hl{``unknowns'' means the separate quantities you are free to
adjust.} A problem with 30 unknowns has 30 dials; one with 1{,}000 has a
thousand. This number --- what the field calls dimension --- is the pivot of
this entire paper.

Algorithms for this work like selective breeding. Keep a population of candidate
answers, combine and adjust them, keep what scores better, discard what scores
worse, repeat until the budget runs out. The skill is in \emph{how} you combine
and adjust: too cautious and you never explore, too adventurous and you never
settle down and refine.

\subsection{The family this work belongs to}

The project builds on \hl{Gaining-Sharing Knowledge} (GSK), a method modelled on
how people learn. Early on you absorb knowledge from a small circle --- people
slightly ahead of and behind you. Later you draw on the most experienced people
you can reach. GSK gives its candidate answers the same two modes: a
\emph{junior} phase learning from near neighbours, and a \emph{senior} phase
learning from the best and worst in the population. Several improved versions
have appeared in recent years, each adjusting how those phases are tuned.

\subsection{The gap}

Every published version shares one habit: it settles on how to behave at a
single operating point. The same dials are used whether the problem has 10
unknowns or 1{,}000.

That is a real weakness, because problem size changes the situation
qualitatively. With 10 unknowns you can afford to examine the landscape
carefully. With 1{,}000 the space is so vast that the same careful machinery
becomes wasted effort --- while different machinery, pointless at 10 unknowns,
starts to pay off. One setting cannot be right for both.

\section{What DT-GSK does differently}

\hl{DT-GSK resolves its behaviour by problem size} --- hence
\emph{Dimension-Tiered}. Think of a gearbox with four gears: under 20 unknowns,
20--49, 50--99, and 100 or more. One important detail: \hl{the gear is chosen
once, from the size of the problem, before the search starts, and never changes
during the run.} A 60-unknown problem simply gets different machinery switched
on than a 15-unknown one.

\subsection{Idea 1: a scaffold that tunes itself}

Instead of fixing its settings in advance, DT-GSK keeps several operating modes
available and learns during the run which ones are producing improvements. Modes
that work get chosen more often; modes that stop working are set aside --- never
permanently, so one that becomes useful again can return. It also shrinks its
population over time, spending early effort on breadth and later effort on
depth, and if progress stalls badly it restarts the search while keeping the
best answer found so far, so a restart can never lose ground.

This scaffold runs \hl{at every problem size}, including the smallest.

\subsection{Idea 2: a deterministic finish}

Most methods in this family end as they began --- randomly. In its final
stretch of budget DT-GSK instead runs a \hl{systematic, repeatable search}
around its best answer: step one way, test, step another way, test, shrink the
step, repeat. No randomness at all. \hl{This switches on only from 50 unknowns
upward}, and fires once, in the last few percent of the budget.

It matters practically as well as scientifically: a random finish gives a
slightly different answer each run, while a deterministic one gives exactly the
same answer every time --- \emph{provided you run it in the same computing
setup the study used}. The paper is explicit that this is not a guarantee across
different hardware or thread settings.

\subsection{Idea 3: a memory of what worked (the honest one)}

The third idea is exploratory. While searching, DT-GSK watches which \emph{pairs}
of unknowns tend to improve together and builds a lightweight map of those
relationships, used to decide which unknowns to change as a group and to orient
the deterministic finish. Like the finish, it is active only from 50 unknowns up.

It needs no extra measurements of the problem --- it is assembled from ones the
algorithm was taking anyway. It is \emph{not} free in computing time, though:
switching it on made runs roughly \hl{30 to 57 percent slower} in the paper's own
timings. And here the paper says something unusual, worth stating plainly:
\hl{tested in isolation, this memory produced no measurable benefit.} The
authors ran the experiment to find out, got a negative answer, and reported it
in the abstract and conclusions rather than burying it. So it cost real time and
bought nothing detectable --- and that is published, not hidden.

\section{How it was tested}

\subsection{Who competed}

Seven algorithms: DT-GSK and \hl{six published members of the same family}
(GSK, AGSK, APGSK, FDB-AGSK, ATMALS-GSK, eGSK). The six were not compared using
numbers copied from their original papers --- they were re-implemented and
re-run inside this project, so every algorithm faced identical problems and
budgets.

\hl{A disclosure the paper makes and this summary repeats:} five of the six
competing methods were written or co-written by two of this paper's own authors,
including the original GSK. No method from outside the GSK family enters any
comparison.

\subsection{On what}

Five standard benchmark collections --- 12{,}265 runs per algorithm, roughly
\hl{86{,}000 runs in total}:

\subsection{Under what rules}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
\item \hl{Equal budget.} Every algorithm was allowed exactly the same number of
      measurements on every problem.
\item \hl{Paired starting points.} On each problem every algorithm used the same
      recorded random seed, and the six comparators shared an identical starting
      set of candidates. \hl{DT-GSK is the one documented exception}: it builds
      its own starting set from that same seed stream, because it uses a
      different population size. The paper flags this as the comparison's one
      fairness asymmetry.
\item \hl{An analysis plan committed in advance --- for one collection.} For
      CEC2020, the authors recorded exactly how they would analyse the results
      before running them, so they could not afterwards pick the flattering
      analysis. This covers CEC2020 only; the other four were analysed without a
      pre-committed plan, and on the very large problems the authors state they
      had already seen the standings before the plan was written.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How results were scored}

Rather than raw numbers, which vary wildly between problems, the study uses
\hl{average rank}: on each problem the seven algorithms are placed 1st to 7th,
and those places are averaged. 1.0 would mean winning everything, 4.0 is exactly
mid-pack, 7.0 would mean losing everything. Separate statistical tests then ask
whether a given gap is real or could plausibly be noise. \hl{The average ranks
below are descriptive summaries --- no statistical test is attached to them.}

\subsection{Reading the wins honestly}

Three bounds apply. \hl{Scope:} DT-GSK is compared against its own family, not
against the strongest optimization methods in the world; the paper makes no
field-wide claim, and on the 1{,}000-unknown problems it says outright that
specialist large-scale methods report substantially better published results than
any member of this family, DT-GSK included. \hl{Statistics:} the first places are
average-rank standings with no test attached --- against its nearest rival eGSK,
DT-GSK's lead on the main collection is inside the margin the tests call noise at
every problem size. \hl{Independence:} CEC2017, the source of the headline first
place, is the collection the settings were chosen on (six candidate
configurations were compared on it before one was frozen), so it should be read
as development performance rather than a fresh test. The tied first on the very
large problems is the weakest of the three: there the tests cannot separate six
of the seven algorithms from one another at all.

\subsection{Reading the losses honestly}

\hl{Second on the real-world formulations.} On CEC2011, eGSK beat DT-GSK, and the
statistical test confirms the gap is real. Note what CEC2011 is: engineering
problems taken from practice and turned into a standard benchmark --- breadth of
application, which the paper distinguishes from evidence of how a method performs
in actual deployment.

\hl{Fourth on the small problems.} On CEC2020, where every problem has 20 or
fewer unknowns, DT-GSK finished fourth of seven. Its two size-gated components
--- the interaction memory and the deterministic finish --- do not switch on
until 50 unknowns, so here it competes without the machinery that earns its wins
higher up; its self-tuning scaffold is still active, so this is a real result
about the method as configured, not an artefact. Before running it the authors
registered the expectation that DT-GSK would \emph{not} lead this collection, and
that same plan required the outcome be reported as a real finding rather than
written off.

\hl{Three confirmed losses, not one.} The CEC2011 result is the only confirmed
loss at the level of a whole collection, but it is not the only one: on the
pre-registered CEC2020 set at 20 unknowns, the same test confirms that both AGSK
and FDB-AGSK beat DT-GSK. Those two sit in the study's highest-weight evidence
tier.

\subsection{The shape of the strength}

The advantage is \hl{tiered, and it is not ``bigger is always better''.} Across
the two mathematical collections spanning 10--100 unknowns, DT-GSK leads at every
size except 30, where it slips to second on CEC2017 and third on CEC2013. On the
small-size collection it leads at none of the four sizes, and on the real-world
set it is second. The strength is concentrated at 50 unknowns and above.

\section{Limits, and why you can check all of this}

\subsection{What the study does not claim}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
\item It does not claim DT-GSK is the best optimizer available --- nor even the
      best method in its own family overall. The paper reports its standing size
      by size rather than as one verdict, and other family members beat it at
      some sizes and on the real-world set.
\item It does not claim the interaction memory works; the direct test came back
      negative and is reported as such.
\item It does not claim faster running times. One competitor, eGSK, could only be
      re-implemented with a substituted internal solver for its final refinement.
      That step spends part of the same budget and can change the answer returned,
      so the eGSK results here are properties of this re-implementation rather
      than of the published algorithm --- and eGSK is the method that beats
      DT-GSK on the real-world set.
\item It does not claim the settings are optimal. They were frozen and never
      re-tuned per problem, with one disclosed exception: two values were added
      for the very-large-problem collection after results on it already existed,
      so that result is not claimed to be outcome-blind.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why the numbers can be checked}

The unusual feature of this project is not the algorithm --- it is the paper
trail:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
\item \hl{Every run is kept}, in read-only frozen data sets, not summarized away.
\item \hl{Results re-derive in the environment the authors specify.} Random
      streams are pinned to recorded seeds, so re-running DT-GSK on a matching
      setup returns the same numbers, not merely similar ones. This guarantee is
      stated for DT-GSK and this project's own pipeline.
\item \hl{Every reported number is traceable} to the data file it came from, with
      automated checks confirming paper and data still agree.
\item \hl{The negative results are published too} --- the component that did not
      work, its compute cost, the loss on the real-world set, the fourth place,
      and the confirmed losses at 20 unknowns.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{If you have to explain DT-GSK in thirty seconds}

\begin{quote}
It is a search method for hard problems where you can test an answer but cannot
calculate the best one directly. Its distinguishing idea is that it picks its own
machinery according to how big the problem is --- like choosing a gear before
setting off --- rather than using one setting for everything, and above a size
threshold it finishes with a systematic, repeatable polish instead of a random
one. Tested against six other published members of \emph{its own algorithm
family} --- not against the strongest optimizers in the field, a comparison the
paper does not make --- across five standard benchmark collections under equal
budgets, it came first on two, tied-first on one, second on one, and fourth on
one. It is weakest on small problems, where its size-gated machinery is switched
off by design, and it loses on the real-world benchmark set. One of its three
components was tested on its own, cost 30--57\% more running time, and showed no
measurable benefit --- and the authors published that result rather than omitting
it.
\end{quote}
